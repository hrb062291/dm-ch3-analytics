Analytics

Analytics isn't a report you glance at. It's how a marketer decides what to do next: which traffic to buy, which page to fix, which channel deserves the budget. Every number in this chapter should end in an action.

The mechanics

How analytics works

When a browser requests a webpage, the server sends back the page code. At the same moment, a small tracking script in that page reports the visit to an analytics server. Nothing is being tested; analytics simply observes what happened. Keep that in mind, because it is the root of the attribution problem later in the chapter.

Cookies

What a cookie is

A small ID stored in the web browser. It is the usual way a site recognizes a returning visitor.

First-party

Set by the site you're visiting. Google Analytics uses a first-party cookie, and the data isn't shared outside your Analytics account.

Third-party

Set by another domain, usually an ad network, so it can follow a person across many sites.

A cookie is unique to one browser on one device, not to one person. Someone who browses on their phone and buys on their laptop shows up as two users unless they log in.
What changed since the textbook
  • Safari and Firefox block third-party cookies by default. Google planned to remove them from Chrome, then reversed course in 2024–2025 and kept them. Cross-site tracking is still much less reliable than it was a decade ago.
  • Privacy rules (GDPR in Europe, state laws in the U.S.) mean many visitors decline analytics cookies. GA4 fills some of the gap with modeled data, so reports are partly estimates.
Objective 1

Choosing KPIs

A website can report hundreds of metrics. A KPI (key performance indicator) is one of the few that tells you whether the business is working. Every site's KPIs fall into three buckets, and multiplying them gives you revenue:

Traffic × Conversion rate × Revenue per conversion = Revenue

The buckets stay the same. What fills them depends on the business model.

The KPI set follows the business model, not the technology. Pages per session is a win for a news site and can be a warning sign for a store (shoppers who can't find what they want click around more).
The most useful number

Revenue per session (RPS)

RPS is often overlooked, but it is the single number that tells you what a visit is worth. That makes it the number to compare against what a visit costs.

Basic formula

RPS = Total revenue ÷ Sessions

$50,000 ÷ 20,000 sessions = $2.50

Alternative formula

RPS = Conversion rate × AOV

(Conversions ÷ Sessions) × (Revenue ÷ Conversions). Same answer, but it shows which lever moved.

Profit per session (PPS)

RPS after the cost of goods sold or service costs. Compare PPS to cost per click (CPC), not RPS. Revenue isn't profit.

Lead generation version

One extra step: RPS = Conversion rate × Close rate × Average deal value. Every rate in the chain compounds.

Why is "direct" the biggest and best channel?

Direct traffic means Analytics couldn't tell where the visitor came from. For a lead-gen business, those visitors are probably people who heard about the company by word of mouth, got a business card, or clicked an untagged link in an email or text message. Objective 3

Attribution: which ad gets the credit?

Jane, an online boutique, runs a banner ad on the New York Times website. A shopper buys from Jane. Did the ad cause the sale? Maybe. The same shopper might also have passed a billboard for Jane's sale, or found "Jane: Daily Boutique Deals" by searching "boutiques near me." Analytics sees only the clicks it can track, and never sees the billboard at all.

The right question: compared to what?

The logic that works

Compare people who saw the ad with people who didn't. If the exposed group buys at a higher rate, the ad had an effect.

The catch: selection bias

Ad platforms show ads to the people most likely to buy anyway. If the two groups differ, you may be measuring good targeting, not a good ad.

The comparison group has to be randomly assigned. Only randomization makes sure the ad, and nothing else, explains the difference.
Low-cost experiment

The placebo ad

Create two ads in the same ad group: your real ad and one for something unrelated (such as a charity). The platform randomly assigns people to one or the other, so you can compare purchase rates. The cost: you pay for the placebo impressions.

Better experiment

"Ghost ads"

Google's method records the people who would have seen your ad and holds the ad back from a random share of them. You get a true control group without paying for irrelevant ads. Today Google offers this as Conversion Lift studies.

Same sales, three rules

Google's sample output credits the same conversions three ways. Last interaction gives all credit to the final click, first interaction to the first, and linear splits credit evenly. Choose a model to see who "wins."

Attribution models don't measure cause. They divide credit by a rule. The model you pick decides which team gets the budget, so know the rule before you defend a budget with it.
What changed since the textbook
  • The textbook's attribution screenshot (the source of these numbers) comes from Universal Analytics, which Google shut down in July 2023.
  • In 2023 Google removed first-click, linear, time-decay and position-based models from GA4 and Google Ads. Today GA4 offers data-driven (the default) and last-click models. The rule-based models are still worth understanding, because other tools use them and they show how much the rule matters.
Practical skills

Setting up tracking

Google Tag Manager

A container for all of a site's tracking tags. The Google tag fires on every page load and sends the hit to Analytics. Marketers can add or change tags without waiting for a developer to redeploy the site.

Link Google Ads

Linking Ads and Analytics lets you see what paid visitors did after the click, and send Analytics conversions back to Ads for bidding.

Link Search Console

Without this link, the organic search queries that brought people to your site never reach Analytics. Paid and organic data stay in separate silos.

Creating a custom event

In GA4, everything a user does is an event. A conversion is simply an event you mark as important.

  1. Go to Admin, then Events.
  2. Click "Create event."
  3. Name it (for example, registration_complete).
  4. Set matching conditions: event_name equals page_view AND page_location equals the thank-you page URL.
  5. Mark the new event as a key event so it is counted as a conversion.

Events built on events

The demo store has an event that fires only when a purchase is over $60. It's built on the existing purchase event, filtered by value.

Where marketing needs engineering

The purchase amount has to be passed into Analytics by the site's code. Plan what you'll measure before the site is built.

What changed since the textbook
  • The "Universal Analytics tag" in Tag Manager is gone. GA4 uses the Google tag.
  • In 2024 GA4 renamed "conversions" to key events. "Conversions" now refers to key events imported into Google Ads.
  • Search Console is linked from GA4 under Admin, then Product links.

Set a comparison date range before you read any number. A metric without a comparison is not analysis.
AcquisitionWhere visitors come from
  • How many users, new users, sessions and engaged sessions were there this month?
  • What is an engaged session? (Lasts 10+ seconds, has a key event, or has 2+ page views.)
  • Which channel had the highest average engagement time? The second highest?
  • Which channel produced the most key events? The highest key event rate? Are they the same channel?
  • Add a comparison for Chrome users. What share of users is on Chrome? Do Chrome users engage more or less than average?
EngagementWhat visitors do
  • Which event occurred most?
  • Which key event occurred most?
  • Which of the top ten pages had the highest average engagement time?
  • Is high engagement time always good? (A confusing page can hold people too.)

The Engagement overview is mostly for orientation. The detail reports are where decisions come from.

MonetizationWas the traffic worth it?
  • Open Ecommerce purchases and add a comparison for mobile users.
  • What share of purchases happened on mobile? What share of item views?
  • What does the gap say about browsing on mobile vs. buying on desktop?
  • Which product produced the most revenue?
AttributionWho gets credit
  • Find the attribution reports (under Advertising) and the conversion paths report.
  • The default model is data-driven: an algorithm guesses which touchpoint "caused" the sale. Why should you be skeptical?
  • Why does so much revenue show up as Direct? What does "Direct × 2" mean?
  • When a path includes both Direct and Paid Search, Paid Search gets all the credit. Why does that make switching models change so little?
People often browse on mobile and buy on desktop. Because cookies are per device, the phone visit and the laptop purchase can look like two different people. That's one more reason last-click reports undercount some channels.
